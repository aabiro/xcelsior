## Product strategy that fits a small team and no hardware

The best product ideas across both reports converge on one principle: **your “platform” is the governance layer that turns heterogeneous third-party GPUs into something enterprises can buy.** You do not need hardware to start—what you need is a control plane, trust controls, and an onboarding motion for hosts and customers.

A cohesive product path that respects your constraints (self-hosted, free/open core, upgrade later) is:

**Build a “trust ladder” with three sellable tiers**, each with clear claims you can actually prove.

### Residency tier as the launchable base

This tier promises: “Your workload is scheduled and executed only on machines physically in Canada.”

Why it’s strong:
- Québec’s Law 25 makes cross-border transfers administratively and legally expensive; a “Québec-only” switch can remove a major compliance burden for many use cases. citeturn0search0turn0search4
- Nova Scotia’s PIIDPA “stored/accessed only in Canada” makes “Canada-only” a hard requirement for many public bodies and their service providers. citeturn2search0turn2search2

How to make it credible (minimum viable proof):
- **Geographic constraints in scheduling** (Canada-only + optional province-tag routing).
- **Operational evidence**: logs showing where jobs ran and which nodes were used (plus the node’s verified metadata).
- **Network posture**: the simplest credible model is a pull-based agent + a private overlay network you control (to avoid random inbound exposure).

### Sovereignty tier as the differentiator

This tier promises: “Workloads run on Canadian-resident infrastructure operated by entities that are under Canadian jurisdiction (or at least meet your published ‘no foreign control’ criteria).”

Why it’s strong:
- Canadian government analysis explicitly says residency alone doesn’t ensure sovereignty from foreign legal reach, and that full legal control is possible only when services are delivered by government itself or providers operating entirely under Canadian jurisdiction. citeturn16search2turn16search0
- The CLOUD Act’s extraterritorial reach is explicit in U.S. code, which reinforces the difference between “Canada region” and “Canada-controlled.” citeturn4search0turn4search9

What this tier requires (and why it’s still feasible for a small team):
- **Policy + vetting**, not just tech: corporate registration evidence, beneficial ownership / control criteria, contractual commitments, and an incident/legal-demand handling framework.
- **Clear, narrow promises**: avoid absolute language (“immune”) and instead publish what you verify and what you do not.

### Regulated tier as the premium expansion (health/public sector)

This tier promises: “Stronger auditability, controls, and reporting suitable for regulated environments.”

Due diligence adjustment:
- In Ontario, the statutory “electronic audit log” provision is drafted but not yet in force, so building a full PHIPA-grade mechanism is likely best positioned as a premium differentiator rather than a launch requirement. citeturn21search0turn21search2
- For PIPEDA, the most concrete fine lever is around breach reporting/record-keeping obligations (up to $100,000 for knowing failure), which supports “strong governance and logs,” but does not by itself mandate Canada-only processing. citeturn5search0turn5search2

A practical implementation insight from your own code direction that is worth keeping (and aligns with Report #1’s security concerns) is “sandbox untrusted workloads.” If you choose to implement a hardened runtime option, gVisor’s documented GPU support via `nvproxy` (with known compatibility constraints) is a legitimate mechanism to reduce host risk from untrusted AI code, and it explicitly notes that consumer GPUs on the same microarchitectures will “likely work” even if not officially listed. citeturn24search0

## Go-to-market strategy and why the subsidy is the key marketing engine

Report #2 is directionally correct that you need pricing and marketing tactics that reduce friction in a cold-start market, but its best contribution becomes far more powerful when married to one program: the AI Compute Access Fund.

What the program changes:
- The AI Compute Access Fund (ISED) covers **two-thirds** of eligible costs for Canadian cloud compute services and **half** for non-Canadian compute services until March 31, 2027; it also defines what qualifies as a “Canadian AI compute service provider,” and sets a cutoff after which non-Canadian compute will no longer be eligible for offsets. citeturn23search0turn1search3
- The program’s call window (most recently closing July 31, 2025) means many SMEs and advisors are actively mapping their compute vendors and invoice structures to meet reimbursement documentation needs—so a vendor that makes compliance paperwork easy can win disproportionately even if raw $/GPU-hour is not the lowest. citeturn1search0turn1search3

The best “marketing plan” idea, therefore, is not generic ads or content—it is this:

**Build and market Xcelsior as the “rebate-ready Canadian compute marketplace.”**

That means:
- Provide customers with **invoices and usage reports that map cleanly to eligible cost categories** (core compute, storage, monitoring, compute-specific security requirements, etc.). citeturn23search0
- Provide a **supplier attestation pack** (your incorporation, where infrastructure runs, how you enforce Canada-only, and how a customer can evidence “costs incurred in Canada”).
- Publish a narrative that connects to the federal intent: the program is part of a broader “Canadian Sovereign AI Compute Strategy,” framed as keeping AI commercialization competitive in Canada. citeturn1search1turn23search2

Pricing tactics worth keeping from Report #2 (with a due-diligence refinement):
- **Price in CAD** and emphasize predictable billing (many competitors price in USD, and Canadian buyers face currency risk and procurement complexity).
- Use **spot/preemptible pricing** as the launch liquidity mechanism (this is consistent with how major GPU rental platforms present pricing tiers; for example, entity["company","Runpod","gpu cloud platform"] publicly lists a large pricing surface including cheaper community-tier 4090 rates and higher secure-tier rates). citeturn13search7turn13search1  
- Don’t anchor the brand on “cheapest compute.” You can’t out-cheap global, low-governance marketplaces indefinitely. Anchor on *“cheapest compliant compute in Canada,”* where compliance is operationalized by your scheduler and your host verification.

A practical enterprise visibility hook:
